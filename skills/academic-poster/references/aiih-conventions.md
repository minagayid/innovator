# AIiH conventions and judging criteria

AIiH (International Conference on AI in Healthcare, aiih.cc) is used as this skill's primary
real-world reference point, because it's one of the few conferences that publishes both its
poster-judging criteria and photos of recent winners. What follows is what its site actually
says, current as of the 2026 edition — re-check aiih.cc directly if working on a submission,
since conference sites change year to year.

## Poster vs. abstract are two different deliverables

AIiH's "Call for Late-Breaking Abstracts" welcomes short abstract submissions (up to 5 pages
including references, LaTeX or Word template) specifically so they can be presented "as poster
or short spot-light presentation at the conference." Accepted abstracts are archived, with a
DOI, on Zenodo (zenodo.org/communities/aiih). What's actually deposited on Zenodo is the written
abstract/paper — a text document — not a rendering of the physical poster. So when someone is
building a poster for AIiH:

- The **abstract** is the content source (motivation, methods, results, conclusion) and is a
  separate, already-formatted document — don't reformat the poster to look like the abstract's
  page layout.
- The **poster** is the physical/display artifact this skill produces, and it isn't itself
  archived anywhere with a DOI.
- Once a DOI exists for the abstract, it's worth adding it to the poster (as a QR code or short
  URL) so visitors can find the fuller written version.

## No published physical poster size

AIiH's abstract, submission-guidelines, and venue pages describe page limits for the *written*
abstract (5 pages) but do not publish a fixed physical poster board size or orientation
requirement anywhere on the public site. This is why this skill defaults to A0 portrait — the
de facto standard at UK and most international academic conferences — rather than assuming a
specific figure. For the 2026 edition, the poster session is held at the Royal Geographical
Society (a short walk from the main venue at Imperial College London); board-sharing and exact
dimensions are the kind of detail that's normally confirmed directly with organisers close to
the date, not published in advance.

## Best Poster Award — judging criteria

Quoting the criteria as published: award selection is "based on scientific quality of the work,
poster design, and presentation delivery." This is a three-part standard, not a design contest
alone — a beautifully designed poster describing weak or unclear science won't win, and neither
will a rigorous study presented as a wall of text. This is the basis for this skill's recommended
hybrid layout: a prominent one-sentence headline finding to carry the "presentation delivery"
component (it's what makes the poster work as a spoken pitch), a compact but genuine IMRaD
backbone for "scientific quality," and one dominant, well-produced visual for "poster design."

Prize structure has varied slightly by year — AIiH 2025's winner received £100 and a certificate;
AIiH 2024's joint winners shared £200 in gift vouchers — presented at the closing session.

## Recent winners, for context on subject matter (not visual design)

The site's award pages include a photo of each year's Best Poster Award winner, but those images
aren't fetchable from this environment — treat any claim about their specific visual layout,
color choices, or typography as unverified, and don't assert it either to yourself or the user.
What's confirmed from the page text is the subject matter and framing that won:

- **2025 winner**: "Integrating Artificial Intelligence to Enhance Clinical Documentation,
  Real-Time Auditing, and Decision-Making in a Tertiary Care Hospital: A Comparative Audit
  Study" (Asfia Akhtar, Faizan Hameed — Bon Secours Hospital) — an applied, hospital-operations
  study with a clear comparative (before/after) framing, which maps naturally onto a
  headline-finding layout.
- **2024 joint winners**: a clustering/trajectory analysis of long-term conditions in adults
  with learning disabilities (Loughborough University et al.), and a zero-knowledge-proof mutual
  authentication scheme for the Internet of Medical Things (Xidian University et al.) — one
  clinical-data-analysis piece and one systems/security piece, suggesting the award rewards
  presentation quality across very different kinds of research rather than favoring one subtype.

The throughline across all three: applied, real-world healthcare framing with a clear
comparative or evaluative angle (audit, before/after, benchmark) rather than purely theoretical
work — useful context when helping a user sharpen their own headline finding, but not a formula
to force onto unrelated work.
