# Peer-review criteria: arguments, plans & proposals

Attack it the way the sharpest person in the room who wants this idea to fail would — not out of malice, but because that's the only way to find out if it actually survives contact with a real objection.

- **Logical fallacies** — check specifically for: strawmanning an opposing view instead of engaging its strongest form, false dichotomies that hide a third option, causal claims resting on correlation alone, survivorship bias (only looking at the cases that worked), appeal to authority in place of evidence, slippery-slope leaps, and circular reasoning where the conclusion is smuggled into a premise.

- **Toulmin check** — for the central argument, confirm each piece is actually present: the **claim** (what's being argued), the **grounds** (the evidence for it), the **warrant** (why the grounds support the claim), and ideally **backing** for the warrant and an acknowledged **rebuttal**. An argument missing a warrant is just an assertion with evidence sitting next to it, not connected to it.

- **Assumption surfacing** — what has to be true for this to work that the piece never actually states? Unstated assumptions are where plans quietly fail, because nobody stress-tested them.

- **Failure-mode / risk analysis** — for plans specifically: what's the first thing that breaks under real conditions, and what's the blast radius when it does? A plan with no failure modes listed hasn't been thought through, it's been hoped through.

- **Feasibility** — are the resources, timeline, and dependencies realistic, or aspirational? Check for dependencies on things outside the plan's control with no contingency.

- **Counterparty reaction** — how would someone who benefits from this plan failing, or who has to execute the boring parts of it, actually respond? A plan that only survives if everyone cooperates enthusiastically is fragile.
